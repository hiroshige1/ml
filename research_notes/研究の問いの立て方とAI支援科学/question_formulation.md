## Key question 1: What canonical scientific-method sources say about choosing important problems and sharpening questions (Hamming, Platt, Polya, Feynman/Rota, Popper, Lakatos, Booth et al., Heilmeier)

### Takeaway
The classic sources converge on a small number of operational moves: keep a standing list of important open problems and test every new tool against it (Hamming, Feynman via Rota); define "important" by whether you have an attack, not by payoff (Hamming); formulate several sharp, disprovable alternatives and design the experiment that kills at least one (Platt/Chamberlin, Popper); prefer bold, high-content conjectures that forbid a lot and predict novel facts (Popper, Lakatos); reshape the problem by generalizing, specializing, varying, or relaxing conditions (Polya); and force the question into a "topic -> question -> so-what" sentence that names who cares (Booth et al., Heilmeier).

### Cited Findings

**Hamming, "You and Your Research" (Bell Communications Research Colloquium, 7 March 1986)**
- Core claim, quoted in snippet: "If you do not work on an important problem, it's unlikely you'll do important work." Minor wording differs across transcripts. — [Hamming transcript (Princeton copy)](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/~jrex/teaching/spring2005/fft/hamming.html); [gwern transcript](https://gwern.net/doc/science/1986-hamming); [Paul Graham copy](https://www.paulgraham.com/hamming.html)
- Hamming defines importance by tractability: time travel, teleportation and antigravity "are not important problems because there is no known way to attack them"; "it's not the consequence that makes a problem important, it is that you have a reasonable attack" (quoted in snippet). — [search summary of transcript](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/~jrex/teaching/spring2005/fft/hamming.html)
- Great scientists "have thought through, in a careful way, a number of important problems in their field, and they keep an eye on wondering how to attack them" (quoted in snippet); eminent scientists keep a list of roughly 10 to 20 important problems and watch for an opening. — [Ten Simple Rules for Doing Your Best Research, According to Hamming (PLoS Comp Bio)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2041981/); [Cal Newport notes](https://calnewport.com/how-to-win-a-nobel-prize-notes-from-richard-hammings-talk-on-doing-great-research/)
- The "open door" passage: closed doors yield more short-term output, but open-door researchers "occasionally pick up clues about what the world is and what might be important"; Hamming reports a strong correlation between open doors and important work while admitting he cannot prove causation. — [Hamming transcript (gwern)](https://gwern.net/doc/science/1986-hamming)
- The talk's question has been turned into a named heuristic, "The Hamming Question" ("What are the important problems in your field, and why aren't you working on them?"), used as a prompt in rationalist/AI-safety circles. — [LessWrong, "The Hamming Question"](https://www.greaterwrong.com/posts/P5k3PGzebd5yYrYqd/the-hamming-question)

**Platt, "Strong Inference", Science 146(3642):347–353, 16 Oct 1964**
- The method is a cycle: (1) devise alternative hypotheses; (2) devise a crucial experiment "with alternative possible outcomes, each of which will, as nearly as possible, exclude one or more of the hypotheses"; (3) carry out the experiment to get a clean result; (1') recycle, refining the surviving possibilities. — [Fifty years of J. R. Platt's strong inference (J Exp Biol 2014)](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/217/8/1202/13095/Fifty-years-of-J-R-Platt-s-strong-inference); [Platt PDF (WHOI mirror)](https://www.whoi.edu/cms/files/platt64sci_72743.pdf); [Platt PDF (CMU mirror)](https://www.stat.cmu.edu/~brian/nynke/726-2007/726-402%20papers%20and%20projects/platt-strong-inference-science-1964.pdf)
- Platt borrowed "multiple working hypotheses" from the geologist T. C. Chamberlin (1897): entertain several hypotheses at once, each "sharp enough to be capable of disproof"; the goal is to disprove the wrong ones rather than confirm a favourite. — [Strong inference: improving the scientific method (path2phd)](https://path2phd.substack.com/p/strong-inference-improving-the-scientific); [Strong Inference for Systems Biology (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2724742/)
- Platt asks (quoted in snippet) "how many of us write down our alternatives and crucial experiments every day, focusing on the exclusion of a hypothesis?" — the daily-notebook discipline. — [search summary](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/F7pihuF8qRbJ6WTue/link-strong-inference)
- Criticism: O'Donohue and Buchanan, "The Weakness of Strong Inference", argue that a complete set of alternatives cannot be enumerated, that Quine–Duhem makes "crucial" experiments logically inconclusive, and that the four steps are ambiguous in execution. — [The weakness of strong inference](https://cornerstone.lib.mnsu.edu/psyc_fac_pubs/37/)
- Platt's paper was reprinted by Science in 1965 and has drawn criticism for idealizing molecular biology and high-energy physics as exemplars. — [ProQuest abstract](https://www.proquest.com/docview/233164946)

**Popper (falsifiability, bold conjectures)**
- Popper's formulation, as quoted by Glöckner and Betsch: "The empirical content of a statement increases with its degree of falsifiability: the more a statement forbids, the more it says about the world of experience." Content can be assessed by universality (Allgemeinheit) and precision (Bestimmtheit). — [Judgment and Decision Making, Dec 2011 (DOAJ)](https://doaj.org/article/6cb3e53a959a441c9ce75bdd2d5bc2af)
- Bold hypotheses that yield novel predictions are risky and hence easier to test, which is why Popper urged boldness; the canonical example is Einstein's prediction that gravity bends starlight, testable at an eclipse. Popper also forbade, as intellectual dishonesty, ad hoc tinkering to save a hypothesis. — [Encyclopedia.com, Falsifiability](https://www.encyclopedia.com/science-and-technology/physics/science-general/falsifiability)
- "Improbabilism": a high-content hypothesis is a priori improbable, easier to refute if false, and more significant if it survives; a cautious claim that survives tells us little because it risked little. — [Philosophy Professor, Improbabilism](https://www.philosophyprofessor.com/philosophies/improbabilism/)

**Lakatos (progressive vs degenerating research programmes)**
- A programme is theoretically progressive if each successive theory has excess empirical content over its predecessor, i.e. "predicts some hitherto unexpected fact" (Lakatos 1978, p. 33), and empirically progressive if that excess content is corroborated ("leads to the actual discovery of some new fact", p. 34). Failing either, it is degenerating: it only offers post-hoc explanations of chance discoveries or of facts a rival anticipated. — [Logic and Logical Philosophy article (apcz.umk.pl)](https://apcz.umk.pl/LLP/article/download/52939/45902/228239); [Methodological Reflections on the MOND/Dark Matter Debate (arXiv 2306.13026)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.13026)
- Lakatos called the anticipation of novel facts "heuristic power", opposed to trial-and-error. Known problems with the criterion: "tacking" (adding an unrelated hypothesis that explains a new fact counts as progressive), no rigorous short-term criterion, and progress often visible only in hindsight. — [arXiv 1703.02389, Cosmology and Convention](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1703.02389); [Notion summary of Lakatos](https://illinxe.notion.site/TPS_Lakatos_231114-91ecd0cf8d2a4f11ab81421b436e44c7)

**Polya, How to Solve It (1945)**
- Heuristic questions for a stalled problem: "If you cannot solve the proposed problem try to solve first some related problem"; look for a more accessible related problem, a more general problem, a more special problem, an analogous problem; "Look at the unknown!" and think of a familiar problem with the same or similar unknown. — [Polya steps handout (UTK)](https://web.eecs.utk.edu/~bmaclenn/Classes/312-S12/handouts/Polya%20How%20to%20Solve%20It%20steps.pdf); [Polya summary (UCR)](https://math.ucr.edu/~res/math133/polya.pdf)
- Variation: "Can you vary or change your problem to create a new problem (or set of problems) whose solution(s) will help you solve your original problem?"; "Keep only a part of the condition, drop the other part; how far is the unknown then determined, how can it vary?" — [Problem solving heuristics wiki (KU)](https://theory.cpe.ku.ac.th/wiki/index.php/Problem_solving_heuristics)
- Inventor's paradox: "a more ambitious problem might be easier to solve" — the general version can be simpler than the narrow original. — [How to Solve It summary (Nathan Wailes)](https://nathanwailes.atlassian.net/wiki/x/wAEb)

**Feynman via Rota, "Ten Lessons I Wish I Had Been Taught" (MIT, April 1996)**
- Rota recounts Feynman's advice: keep "a dozen of your favorite problems" constantly present in mind, mostly dormant; test every new trick or result against all twelve; occasionally one hits and people say "How did he do it? He must be a genius!" The wording is Rota's recollection, not Feynman's published text; sources vary between "dozen" and "twelve". — [Forte Labs, 12 Favorite Problems](https://fortelabs.com/blog/12-favorite-problems-how-to-spark-genius-with-the-power-of-open-questions/); [Art of Manliness](https://www.artofmanliness.com/character/self-improvement/think-like-feynman-why-you-should-have-12-favorite-problems/)
- Fermi's dictum, quoted in a workshop deck: "If the result confirms the hypothesis, then you've made a measurement. If the result is contrary to the hypothesis, then you've made a discovery." — [Peter Stuckey, IMDEA experimental-methods slides](https://people.eng.unimelb.edu.au/pstuckey/imdea_exp.pdf)

**Booth, Colomb & Williams, The Craft of Research**
- The three-step formula (Part 2, cited at p. 51 of an earlier edition) ties a question to its significance: "I am studying [topic], because I want to find out [question], in order to help my reader understand [significance]." A reviewer calls it a "tool for thinking" about the research enterprise. The 5th edition (2024) phrases the last slot as "audience". — [Review of Higher Education via Project MUSE](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/254815); [Craft of Research 5th ed. summary](https://businesssciencedaily.com/the-craft-of-research-booth-et-al-5th-ed-2024/); [Cliffnotes to Craft of Research](https://www.greaterwrong.com/posts/fvJECtgMX4cP62t2s/cliffnotes-to-craft-of-research-parts-i-ii-and-iii)

**Heilmeier Catechism (DARPA)**
- DARPA's official list: (1) What are you trying to do? Articulate your objectives using absolutely no jargon. (2) How is it done today, and what are the limits of current practice? (3) What is new in your approach and why do you think it will be successful? (4) Who cares? If you are successful, what difference will it make? (5) What are the risks? (6) How much will it cost? (7) How long will it take? (8) What are the mid-term and final "exams" to check for success? — [DARPA, Heilmeier Catechism](https://www.darpa.mil/about/heilmeier-catechism); [CASRAI guide](https://casrai.org/guides/heilmeier-catechism)
- Variants: some versions split Q5 into "risks" and "payoffs" (nine questions); another adds "What is the problem? Why is it hard?" which is not in DARPA's list. Heilmeier ran DARPA 1975–77 and reportedly introduced the questions to filter proposals. — [Janert, The Heilmeier Catechism](https://janert.me/blog/2022/the-heilmeier-catechism/); [Palindrome Tech history](https://palindrometech.com/applied-security-research-blog/george-h.-heilmeier-architect-of-innovation-and-the-enduring-catechism)

**FINER (Hulley et al., Designing Clinical Research)**
- The FINER screen for a research question: Feasible (adequate subjects, expertise, time, money, manageable scope), Interesting, Novel (confirms/refutes/extends prior findings or produces new ones), Ethical, Relevant (to scientific knowledge, to policy, to future research). Extended version FINERMAPS adds Manageable, Appropriate, Potential value/publishability, Systematic. Several authors stress a study should have one primary question. — [Wikiversity, Research question](https://en.wikiversity.org/wiki/Research_question); [PMC2912019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2912019); [Criteria for a good research topic (Cirugía Española)](https://www.elsevier.es/en-revista-cirugia-espanola-english-edition--436-articulo-criteria-for-good-research-topic--S2173507722000783)

### Inferences
- Combining Hamming and Popper gives a two-sided filter for an "incremental" idea: it fails Hamming if there is no new attack (nothing you can do that others couldn't), and it fails Popper if its answer forbids nothing (any outcome is compatible with the current picture).
- Platt's cycle translates directly into a test for ML questions: write down at least two live hypotheses an expert might hold about the phenomenon, and the experiment (or theorem) whose outcomes are mutually exclusive between them. If you cannot name the second hypothesis, the question is confirmatory, not discriminating.
- Polya's "inventor's paradox" and "drop part of the condition" are the mathematically native versions of the ML move "strip the setting down to the toy model that still exhibits the phenomenon" (see Key question 5).
- Lakatos' "novel fact" criterion is the sharpest anti-incrementality test among the classics: a contribution that merely re-explains known facts in new notation is, in his vocabulary, a degenerating problemshift.
- From memory (not verifiable this session): Hamming's talk also discusses "courage", "compound interest" of effort, working with the door open, and the advice to "study the problem of how to work on important problems"; Platt's paper also discusses "The Question" ("But sir, what experiment could disprove your hypothesis?") and the distinction between method-oriented and problem-oriented science. Treat these as unconfirmed.

## Key question 2: What ML-specific guides say (Schulman, Olah/Distill, Nanda, Steinhardt, Lipton & Steinhardt, Nielsen, Tao, Barak, Arora, Peyton Jones, venue guidelines)

### Takeaway
ML-specific advice adds three things to the classics: (a) choose goal-driven over idea-driven problems because literature-driven ideas are reachable by everyone reading the same papers (Schulman); (b) treat the research project as a sequence of information-gathering steps and do the most informative, riskiest-assumption-killing step first (Steinhardt); (c) demand that explanations be separated from speculation, that gains be attributed by ablation, and that math clarify rather than impress (Lipton & Steinhardt), with venue guidelines explicitly rewarding theory that "sheds light on empirical phenomena" (COLT) and asking theory papers to discuss practical utility (JMLR).

### Cited Findings

**John Schulman, "An Opinionated Guide to ML Research" (written Dec 2017 for OpenAI Fellows, published 2020)**
- Two ways to pick problems. Idea-driven: "You follow some sector of the literature. As you read a paper showing how to do X, you get an idea for doing X even better, and then you start a project to test that idea." Goal-driven: "You develop a vision of some new AI capabilities you'd like to achieve, and solve problems that bring you closer to that goal"; the goal is more like "make X work for the first time" than "make X work better". — [joschu.net (original)](http://joschu.net/blog/opinionated-guide-ml-research.html); [mirror](https://jeonsworld.github.io/Research/ml_research/)
- Schulman recommends goal-driven research for most people and says he mostly followed it himself; idea-driven work risks being scooped because researchers worldwide read the same literature. Quoted in snippet: "To make breakthroughs with idea-driven research, you need to develop an exceptionally deep understanding of your subject, and a perspective that diverges from the rest of the community—some can do it, but it's hard." — [hardmaru quoting the guide](https://x.com/hardmaru/status/1223057947237875712)
- The section on choosing problems opens by saying the ability to pick the right problems matters even more than technical skill; taste develops "as you see which ideas prosper and which are forgotten". Reader notes record three forms of ultimate goal (a groundbreaking result, a reusable algorithmic idea, a deep insight about recurring questions), the advice to "ask questions nobody else is asking", and to prefer solutions general enough to transfer. — [Opinionated Reference on Doing Research (reader notes)](https://tossatree.bearblog.dev/research/); [Schulman slides, "How to do research" (PDF)](https://pengsida.net/files/John_Schulman_How_to_do_research.pdf)
- Notebook practice: keep a research notebook, review it every 1–2 weeks and condense into a summary (experimental findings, insights, code progress, next steps); a flagged common mistake is switching problems too often. — [Opinionated Reference on Doing Research](https://tossatree.bearblog.dev/research/)

**Chris Olah & Shan Carter, "Research Debt" (Distill, 2017)**
- "Research debt is the accumulation of missing interpretive labor" (quoted in snippet); examples are poor exposition, undigested ideas, bad abstractions and notation. Explanation is a tradeoff "between the energy put into explaining an idea, and the energy needed to understand it". Distillation is "the opposite of research debt" and combines "deep scientific understanding, empathy, and design to do justice to our research and lay bare beautiful insights". — [Distill, Research Debt](https://distill.pub/2017/research-debt/); [EA Forum repost/summary](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/EbvJRAvwtKAMBn2td/distillation-and-research-debt)
- Olah (80,000 Hours, 2021) argued academia does not incentivise lucid interpretations of complex ideas, and founded Distill to explain existing results unusually clearly. — [80,000 Hours #108](https://80000hours.org/podcast/episodes/chris-olah-unconventional-career-path/)

**Neel Nanda, research-process sequence (2025)**
- Four-stage process: ideation (choosing a problem/domain), exploration (gaining surface area), understanding (testing hypotheses), distillation (compressing and communicating). — [How I Think About My Research Process: Explore, Understand, Distill](https://www.alignmentforum.org/posts/hjMy4ZxS5ogA9cTYK/how-i-think-about-my-research-process-explore-understand)
- Taste is "much broader than picking the right problem": it covers "identifying which anomalies are and are not worth exploring, distinguishing an experiment that will be compelling from one that'll have inconclusive results" (quoted in snippet). Taste "can be just gut feeling, but also having conceptual frameworks you reason through"; "talent plays an important role, but taste is largely learned". He distinguishes short-feedback skills from long-feedback "strategic taste" (problem choice) whose feedback "might take months or even years". — [My Research Process: Understanding and Cultivating Research Taste](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/Ldrss6o3tiKT6NdMm/my-research-process-understanding-and-cultivating-research); [GreaterWrong mirror](https://www.greaterwrong.com/posts/Ldrss6o3tiKT6NdMm/my-research-process-understanding-and-cultivating-research)
- Practical: in the quickstart he advises not to overthink problem choice — skim options, pick one, run with it; the two most important things are "finding a problem you're excited about and having a sense of the basic techniques you'd use on it". His current selection heuristic is pragmatic: identify what stands between today and AGI-relevant goals, then focus on "the subset of resulting research problems that can be tractably studied on today's models". — [Mechanistic Interpretability Quickstart](https://www.neelnanda.io/mechanistic-interpretability/quickstart-old); [MATS stream page](https://www.matsprogram.org/stream/nanda)
- An auto-generated (unverified) summary lists ways to speed up taste: use mentors, analyse failures, keep a research log, predict feedback before receiving it. — [EA Forum crosspost](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/puEjjCAHkbrLz8nbc/my-research-process-understanding-and-cultivating-research)

**Jacob Steinhardt, "Research as a Stochastic Decision Process" (Dec 2018)**
- Framing: research on uncertain problems is a decision process in which each step may fail; aim to maximise probability of eventual success while minimising expected time. Recommendations: ordering tasks by difficulty or by quickest completion is often counterproductive; rank tasks by information gained per unit time (expected time saved, failure rate); de-risk early via prototypes, simulations, experiments. Steinhardt reports "little research progress for over a year" before adopting it, then "one project every four months on average", and expects it to at least double a reader's productivity. — [Steinhardt homepage essay list](https://www.stat.berkeley.edu/~jsteinhardt/index.html); [Stafforini summary](https://stafforini.com/works/steinhardt-2018-research-as-stochastic/); [Lessons from Jacob Steinhardt (notes)](https://www.antoinebuteau.com/lessons-from-jacob-steinhardt/)

**Lipton & Steinhardt, "Troubling Trends in Machine Learning Scholarship" (arXiv 1807.03341, 2018; ACM Queue 2019)**
- Four trends: (i) failure to distinguish between explanation and speculation, (ii) failure to identify the sources of empirical gains, (iii) mathiness, (iv) misuse of language. Example of (ii): emphasising architectural modifications when gains stem from hyper-parameter tuning. — [ar5iv HTML](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1807.03341); [ACM Queue version](https://queue.acm.org/detail.cfm?id=3328534)
- Remedies: focus on "how and why" a method works rather than raw performance; do error analysis, ablation studies and robustness checks; quarantine speculation (the dropout paper's "Motivation" section is cited as a positive model). Reviewer self-check, quoted in snippet: "Might I have accepted this paper if the authors had done a worse job?" Mathiness (term from Paul Romer) is "the use of mathematics that obfuscates or impresses rather than clarifies". — [dair.ai overview](https://dair.ai/posts/An_Overview_of_Troubling_Trends_in_Machine_Learning_Scholarship); [Montreal AI Ethics summary](https://montrealethics.ai/research-summary-troubling-trends-in-machine-learning-scholarship)

**Michael Nielsen, "Principles of Effective Research" (UQ Technical Note 0404, 27 July 2004)**
- Two idealised styles. Problem-solver: "works intensively on well-posed technical problems, often problems known (and sometimes well-known) to the entire research community ... often attach great social cache to the level of difficulty of the problem they solve, without necessarily worrying so much about other indicators of the importance of the problem." Problem-creator: "a rarer working style ... may often write papers that are technically rather simple, but ask an interesting new question, or pose an old problem in a new way." The problem-solver is "much more passive", the problem-creator "extremely proactive". — [Nielsen blog excerpt](https://michaelnielsen.org/blog/?p=114); [PDF (BU mirror)](https://www.bumc.bu.edu/preventive-med/files/2020/06/Principls-of-effective-reserache-nielsen-0404.pdf)
- Secondary summary of Nielsen's advice for becoming a problem-creator: look at areas that are confusing, because confusion usually means the right foundational concepts have not been found yet. — [Lessons from Michael Nielsen (notes)](https://www.antoinebuteau.com/lessons-from-michael-nielsen/)

**Simon Peyton Jones, "How to write a great research paper"**
- A paper should carry "one clear, sharp idea" — one "ping"; you may not know the ping when you start writing but must know it by the end; many papers contain good ideas without distilling what they are. - "Research looks for 'Surprise'" — ideas that challenge common wisdom and extend what is known — with the caveat that a surprise eventually becomes the new expectation. — [Kaiming He slides (PDF)](https://people.csail.mit.edu/kaiming/neurips2024workshop/neurips2024_newinml_kaiming.pdf)

### Inferences
- Schulman's goal-driven vs idea-driven split is the ML-native diagnosis of "my proposal feels incremental": an idea generated by reading paper X and improving X is by construction reachable by every reader of X. The cure he implies is to start from a capability or phenomenon you want to explain or achieve and derive the question backwards.
- Nielsen's problem-creator profile says the technically simple paper that asks a new question or reposes an old one is a legitimate and higher-leverage contribution type — directly relevant to theorists who feel their technical result is "small".
- Steinhardt's information-per-unit-time rule applies to question formulation itself: the sub-question to attack first is the one whose answer most changes your belief about whether the whole project is worth doing (usually the one most likely to kill it).
- Lipton & Steinhardt's "would I have accepted this if the authors had done a worse job?" can be inverted into an author-side question: "would the paper still be worth reading if the theorem were stated without the surrounding mathiness?"
- JMLR's "discuss practical utility" plus COLT's "sheds light on empirical phenomena" indicates that, at theory venues, a theory question is judged partly by which empirical regularity it explains or which design decision it informs.

- Nanda's post 3 lists concrete "tricks to speed up learning" taste (per his tweet) but the retrieved text cut off before them; only an auto-generated summary was available.

## Key question 3: Concrete tests that distinguish a strong question from an incremental one

### Takeaway
Across sources, a strong question (a) has a concrete attack (Hamming), (b) admits at least two live hypotheses and an experiment/theorem that discriminates them (Platt), (c) forbids outcomes — an expert could lose a bet on it (Popper, Fermi, Sunder, Kaiming He), (d) predicts a novel fact rather than re-explaining known ones (Lakatos), (e) can be stated jargon-free with a named beneficiary (Heilmeier Q1, Q4; Booth "in order to help my reader understand"), and (f) is phrased so the first experiment is the most informative, not the easiest (Steinhardt). Below the findings are organised as a usable checklist, each item tied to its source.

### Cited Findings
- Attack test: a problem is important only if "you have a reasonable attack"; otherwise it is in the time-travel/antigravity class. — [Hamming transcript](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/~jrex/teaching/spring2005/fft/hamming.html)
- Discrimination test: write "alternative hypotheses" and "a crucial experiment with alternative possible outcomes, each of which will, as nearly as possible, exclude one or more of the hypotheses"; do this in a notebook every day. — [Fifty years of strong inference](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/217/8/1202/13095/Fifty-years-of-J-R-Platt-s-strong-inference)
- Content test: "the more a statement forbids, the more it says about the world of experience"; prefer bold conjectures that are improbable a priori. — [Glöckner & Betsch quoting Popper](https://doaj.org/article/6cb3e53a959a441c9ce75bdd2d5bc2af)
- Surprise test (experimental economics, Shyam Sunder's seven questions as quoted in a slide deck): "What are the chances that the answer you get from the experiment will surprise you or others? What are the chances that it will change someone's mind?" — with the note that confirming previous results also matters. — [Hennig-Schmidt deck (HSE mirror)](https://epee.hse.ru/mirror/pubs/share/direct/298823986.pdf)
- Measurement-vs-discovery test (Fermi, as quoted): confirming the hypothesis is a measurement, contradicting it is a discovery. — [Stuckey slides](https://people.eng.unimelb.edu.au/pstuckey/imdea_exp.pdf)
- "Research looks for 'Surprise'": challenge common wisdom; a surprise later becomes the new expectation. — [Kaiming He NeurIPS 2024 slides](https://people.csail.mit.edu/kaiming/neurips2024workshop/neurips2024_newinml_kaiming.pdf)
- Bug-before-breakthrough caveat (Leo Gao): "Any shocking or surprising result in your own experiment is 80% likely to be a bug until proven otherwise." — [LessWrong shortform](https://www.greaterwrong.com/posts/YiRsCfkJ2ERGpRpen/leogao-s-shortform/comment/FBNnKe7i5imhFfJxh)
- Novel-fact test: progressive only if the theory "predicts some hitherto unexpected fact" and that fact is then found; post-hoc accommodation is degenerating. — [LLP article on Lakatos](https://apcz.umk.pl/LLP/article/download/52939/45902/228239)
- Jargon-free statement and "Who cares?" tests. — [DARPA Heilmeier Catechism](https://www.darpa.mil/about/heilmeier-catechism)
- Topic/question/significance sentence test. — [Craft of Research review](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/254815)
- Informativeness-first ordering of sub-questions; de-risk the riskiest assumption first. — [Steinhardt essay (summary)](https://stafforini.com/works/steinhardt-2018-research-as-stochastic/)
- Attribution test: can you say what produced the gain/phenomenon (ablation, error analysis, robustness), and is speculation quarantined from explanation? — [Lipton & Steinhardt via dair.ai](https://dair.ai/posts/An_Overview_of_Troubling_Trends_in_Machine_Learning_Scholarship)
- Reachability test (Schulman): an idea obtained by reading paper X and improving X is one others reading X will also have; prefer goals of the form "make X work for the first time". — [Opinionated Guide](http://joschu.net/blog/opinionated-guide-ml-research.html)
- One-ping test: can the contribution be stated as a single sharp idea? — [Peyton Jones via ISCP](https://www.iscpresearch.org/resources/how-to-write-a-great-research-paper/)
- Anomaly-selection test (Nanda): taste includes "identifying which anomalies are and are not worth exploring" and distinguishing "an experiment that will be compelling from one that'll have inconclusive results". — [Nanda, Research Taste](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/Ldrss6o3tiKT6NdMm/my-research-process-understanding-and-cultivating-research)
- Practical-utility test for theory: JMLR asks theory papers to "discuss their practical utility"; COLT names "theory that sheds light on empirical phenomena" as in scope. — [JMLR reviewer guide](https://www.jmlr.org/beta/reviewer-guide.html); [COLT 2026 CfP](https://learningtheory.org/colt2026/cfp.html)
- Course-level synthesis: the Bau Lab "neural mechanics" course page tells students to ask "is this the right question?" before investing months, explicitly drawing on Hamming and on methodology literature from other fields. — [Bau Lab course, Week 0](https://neural-mechanics.baulab.info/week0_research.html)
- A rationalist operationalisation of Hamming: "What are the important problems in your field, and why aren't you working on them?" — [The Hamming Question](https://www.greaterwrong.com/posts/P5k3PGzebd5yYrYqd/the-hamming-question)

### Inferences (a consolidated checklist; each item traces to the findings above)
A question is probably still incremental if it fails two or more of these:
1. Two-hypothesis test (Platt): name the second hypothesis a competent expert holds. State the outcome under each. If both hypotheses predict the same outcome, the experiment/theorem discriminates nothing.
2. Bet test (Popper/Fermi/Sunder): would a senior person in the subfield take the other side of a bet on the outcome at even odds? If no one would, the result is a measurement, not a discovery.
3. Forbidding test (Popper): write down one concrete observation or theorem that would falsify your claim. If the claim survives every conceivable outcome ("deep nets exploit structure"), it has no content.
4. Novel-fact test (Lakatos): does the proposal predict something not yet measured (a number, an exponent, a crossover point, a monotonicity, a failure mode) — or does it only re-derive what is known in a new setting?
5. Attack test (Hamming): what specifically can you do now that could not be done last year (new tool, new data, new regime, new reduction)? If the answer is "nothing", the problem is either unimportant to you or not yet attackable.
6. Reachability test (Schulman): would ten other people reading the same two papers formulate the same question? If yes, you are competing on speed, not insight; rederive the question from a goal or phenomenon instead.
7. Who-cares test (Heilmeier Q4 / Booth): complete "in order to help [specific audience] understand/decide [X]". If the beneficiary is "the community", the significance is unarticulated.
8. One-ping test (Peyton Jones): state the contribution in one sentence without "and".
9. Jargon test (Heilmeier Q1): state the question with no field-specific terms. Difficulty here usually means the question is about the formalism, not the phenomenon.
10. First-experiment test (Steinhardt): is the first thing you will do the thing most likely to kill the project? If the first step is the easiest, reorder.
11. Attribution test (Lipton & Steinhardt): if the answer comes out positive, will you be able to say which component produced it?
12. Confusion test (Nielsen): is the question located where the field is confused (no agreed concepts yet)? Problem-creator contributions live there.

### Gaps
- I did not find a primary ML source stating the heuristic "what would surprise an expert" in those words; the closest documented statements are Kaiming He's "research looks for surprise", Sunder's experimental-economics questions, and Fermi's measurement/discovery line.
- No source was found that empirically validates any of these tests (e.g., by correlating them with citation impact); they are expert heuristics, not measured predictors.

## Key question 4: How theorists convert a "known result in a new setting" into a genuine contribution

### Takeaway
The documented routes are: (1) explain an empirical anomaly the standard theory cannot (Zhang et al. random labels; Belkin double descent); (2) exhibit a new phenomenon or mechanism and a phase diagram/progress measure for it (grokking phases, induction-head phase change); (3) derive a falsifiable quantitative prediction from a toy model and check it on real systems (quantization model's power-law exponent; Schaeffer's metric predictions); (4) reframe an old question so it becomes a new problem (Nielsen's problem-creator; Polya's variation). Venue criteria make explicit that "formulating a new problem" and "theory that sheds light on empirical phenomena" count as contributions.

### Cited Findings
- ICML lists "connecting ideas in disparate fields, or formulating a new problem" as valid contribution types alongside theoretical and empirical ones. — [ICML 2020 Reviewer Guidelines](https://ICML.cc/Conferences/2020/ReviewerGuidelines)
- COLT: "theory that sheds light on empirical phenomena" is in scope; JMLR: theory should "discuss practical utility"; NeurIPS asks whether claims are supported and whether authors are honest about weaknesses. — [COLT 2026 CfP](https://learningtheory.org/colt2026/cfp.html); [JMLR reviewer guide](https://www.jmlr.org/beta/reviewer-guide.html); [NeurIPS 2026 Reviewer Guidelines](https://neurips.cc/Conferences/2026/ReviewerGuidelines)
- Zhang, Bengio, Hardt, Recht, Vinyals (ICLR 2017): "state-of-the-art convolutional networks easily fit a random labeling of the training data ... qualitatively unaffected by explicit regularization, and occurs even if the true images are replaced by completely unstructured random noise"; plus a theoretical result that depth-two networks have "perfect finite sample expressivity as soon as the number of parameters exceeds the number of data points". The claimed contribution is negative: "the traditional view of generalization is incapable of distinguishing between different neural networks that have radically different generalization performance." Republished in CACM 2021; one reviewer reportedly found the results "completely unsurprising". — [arXiv 1611.03530](https://arxiv.org/abs/1611.03530v1); [Princeton COS597A lecture notes](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/courses/archive/fall17/cos597A/lecnotes/rethinkinggeneralization.pdf); [UBC MLRG slides](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/labs/lci/mlrg/slides/understanding_deep_learning.pdf); [SSRC citation record](https://just-tech.ssrc.org/citation/understanding-deep-learning-requires-rethinking-generalization/)
- Belkin, Hsu, Ma, Mandal (PNAS 2019): the "double descent" curve "subsumes the textbook U-shaped bias-variance trade-off curve by showing how increasing model capacity beyond the point of interpolation results in improved performance"; introduces the "interpolation threshold"; shown for neural nets and random forests. — [arXiv 1812.11118](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1812.11118); [NSF PAR copy](https://par.nsf.gov//servlets/purl/10204300)
- Nakkiran et al. (ICLR 2020) extend it: double descent in model size and in training epochs, unified by "effective model complexity"; "in some regimes, increasing (even quadrupling) the number of train samples actually hurts test performance". Follow-up theory (d'Ascoli, Refinetti, Biroli, Krzakala 2020) decomposes the peak into sampling, label-noise and initialization variances in random-features regression and shows ensembling suppresses them. — [arXiv 1912.02292](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1912.02292); [Double Trouble in Double Descent (arXiv 2003.01054)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2003.01054)
- Arora's 2021 framing: black-box treatment "can miss special phenomena during training" not captured by the objective; understanding them mathematically is "crucial". — [Tsinghua IIIS abstract](https://iiis.tsinghua.edu.cn/en/info/1044/1814.htm)
- Nielsen: problem-creators "may often write papers that are technically rather simple, but ask an interesting new question, or pose an old problem in a new way". — [Nielsen blog](https://michaelnielsen.org/blog/?p=114)
- Polya's reformulation moves (generalize, specialize, vary, drop part of the condition; "a more ambitious problem might be easier to solve"). — [KU heuristics wiki](https://theory.cpe.ku.ac.th/wiki/index.php/Problem_solving_heuristics)
- Tao's interrogation of a known result: alternate proofs, why each hypothesis is necessary, known/conjectured generalizations, weaker versions sufficient for applications. — [AMS Notices Jan 2024](https://www.ams.org/journals/notices/202401/rnoti-p30.pdf)

### Inferences
- The pattern in the influential cases is: known theory T predicts P in setting S; a cheap experiment or toy model in S shows not-P (random labels fit; test error falling past interpolation; loss smooth while accuracy jumps). The contribution is not the new setting but the demonstration that T's explanatory scope is smaller than believed — a Popperian refutation plus a Lakatosian "novel fact".
- Tao's four questions are a procedure for turning "known result, new setting" into a real question: ask which hypothesis of the known result the new setting violates; if none, the transfer is routine; if one, the contribution is to characterise what happens when that hypothesis fails (separation, phase transition, or new rate).
- Venue criteria imply two acceptable framings for a transferred result: (i) it informs a practitioner decision (JMLR practical utility), or (ii) it explains a measured regularity (COLT). A transferred bound that does neither is what reviewers call incremental.
- From memory, not verifiable this session: the ML-theory community's typical "upgrade" moves are proving a separation (what one class can do and another provably cannot), identifying a phase diagram in (data, width, noise, time), proving a tight rate with matching lower bound, or showing an impossibility. I found no single source enumerating these; treat as unsourced synthesis.

### Gaps
- No document was found that explicitly teaches theorists how to upgrade "known result in new setting" into a contribution; the above is inferred from exemplary papers and reviewer criteria.
- Could not verify whether Zhang et al. received an ICLR 2017 best-paper award (a slide deck claims it; official confirmation not retrieved).

## Key question 5: Documented examples where a toy model + sharp question produced a high-impact ML theory/science result, and what made the question good

### Takeaway
The cases share a template: isolate one puzzling regularity (sudden generalization; sudden in-context learning; emergent abilities; power-law loss), shrink it to a system small enough to fully analyse (modular arithmetic transformer; 2-layer attention-only models; a fixed model family with the metric varied; a synthetic multitask dataset), form a mechanistic or statistical hypothesis, and derive predictions that are then checked on larger systems. Each question was discriminating (Platt), surprising to prevailing belief (Popper/He), and produced a quantitative or causal prediction (Lakatos).

### Cited Findings

**Grokking: Nanda, Chan, Lieberum, Smith, Steinhardt, "Progress measures for grokking via mechanistic interpretability" (ICLR 2023 oral)**
- Setting: small transformers on modular addition. The learned algorithm "uses discrete Fourier transforms and trigonometric identities to convert addition to rotation about a circle", confirmed via activations, weights, and Fourier-space ablations. Grokking is decomposed into three continuous phases — memorization, circuit formation, cleanup — and progress measures are defined for them. Conclusion: grokking "arises from the gradual amplification of structured mechanisms encoded in the weights, followed by the later removal of memorizing components", rather than a sudden shift. — [arXiv 2301.05217](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.05217v3); [ICLR 2023 oral page](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2023/oral/12572)

**Induction heads: Olsson, Elhage, Nanda, ... Olah, "In-context Learning and Induction Heads" (Transformer Circuits, 2022)**
- "Six complementary lines of evidence" that induction heads "may be the mechanistic source of general in-context learning in transformer models of any size". Key observation: induction heads "develop at precisely the same point as a sudden sharp increase in in-context learning ability, visible as a bump in the training loss", early in training for every model with more than one layer. Causal perturbation: altering the architecture moves the bump and "the formation of induction heads as well as formation of in-context learning simultaneously move along with it". Strength is graded: "For small attention-only models, we present strong, causal evidence; for larger models with MLPs, we present correlational evidence"; authors call it "only the beginnings of evidence". — [Transformer Circuits page](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2022/in-context-learning-and-induction-heads/); [arXiv 2209.11895](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.11895)

**Emergence as metric artifact: Schaeffer, Miranda, Koyejo, "Are Emergent Abilities of Large Language Models a Mirage?" (NeurIPS 2023 oral)**
- Hypothesis: emergent abilities "can be an artifact of the metric the researcher picks, not a fundamental change in model behavior with scale"; nonlinear/discontinuous metrics produce apparent emergence, linear/continuous metrics "produce smooth, continuous predictable changes". Procedure: build a simple mathematical model, then "make, test and confirm three predictions on the effect of metric choice using the InstructGPT/GPT-3 family", "make, test and confirm two predictions about metric choices in a meta-analysis of emergent abilities on BIG-Bench", and "show how to choose metrics to produce seemingly emergent abilities in multiple vision tasks". Conclusion: alleged emergent abilities "evaporate with different metrics or with better statistics". Presented as a NeurIPS 2023 oral; the search did not confirm an award. — [arXiv 2304.15004](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.15004v1); [NeurIPS 2023 oral listing](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2023/oral/73863); [NeurIPS proceedings PDF](https://papers.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/file/adc98a266f45005c403b8311ca7e8bd7-Paper-Conference.pdf)

**Quanta: Michaud, Liu, Girit, Tegmark, "The Quantization Model of Neural Scaling" (NeurIPS 2023)**
- Aims to explain two observations at once: smooth power-law loss scaling and abrupt emergence. "Quantization Hypothesis": knowledge/skills decompose into discrete "quanta". Central prediction: if quanta are learned in order of use frequency and frequencies follow a power law, loss scales as a power law; aggregate loss looks smooth while individual tasks jump. Validated on toy datasets, then language-model gradients are used to cluster behaviour into skills whose frequencies "roughly follow a power law whose exponent matches the empirical scaling exponent for language models"; authors call the LLM finding tentative. — [arXiv 2303.13506](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.13506v3); [NeurIPS 2023 abstract](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/hash/5b6346a05a537d4cdb2f50323452a9fe-Abstract.html)

**Random labels: Zhang et al. (ICLR 2017)** — see Key question 4: a one-line experiment (randomize labels) that the prevailing theory predicted should fail.

**Double descent: Belkin et al. (PNAS 2019), Nakkiran et al. (ICLR 2020)** — see Key question 4: a plot of test error past the interpolation threshold that the textbook curve forbade.

**Methodological framing from the sources**
- Platt's crucial-experiment logic (outcomes that exclude hypotheses) and Popper's "forbids" criterion are the general principles these papers instantiate. — [J Exp Biol on Platt](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/217/8/1202/13095/Fifty-years-of-J-R-Platt-s-strong-inference); [Glöckner & Betsch](https://doaj.org/article/6cb3e53a959a441c9ce75bdd2d5bc2af)
- Lipton & Steinhardt's demand to separate explanation from speculation is visibly honoured in the induction-heads paper's graded evidence language and the quanta paper's "tentative" label. — [ar5iv 1807.03341](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1807.03341)

### Inferences (what made each question good)
- Grokking: the question was not "why does grokking happen?" but "what is the algorithm, and can we define a continuous quantity that moves while test accuracy is flat?" — converting a discrete mystery into a measurable progress variable. It passes the two-hypothesis test (sudden phase transition vs gradual hidden progress) and yields a novel fact (three phases, cleanup driven by weight decay).
- Induction heads: the question was tied to a specific, universal, reproducible anomaly (the loss bump) and asked for a mechanism that would move with it under intervention — a causal discrimination test on a toy (attention-only) model, then honestly downgraded to correlation at scale.
- Emergence mirage: the question held the model fixed and varied the measurement — a classic Polya "drop part of the condition" move — and produced five pre-registered-style predictions plus a constructive demonstration (manufacturing emergence in vision). It is a Lakatosian "novel fact" because it predicted where emergence would and would not appear before measuring.
- Quanta: the question demanded one mechanism that explains two apparently contradictory regularities (smooth power laws and discrete jumps) and predicted a number (the exponent) that could match or fail to match LLM data.
- Random labels and double descent: both are "what does the textbook forbid, and can I observe it cheaply?" questions; the attack was trivially available, which is exactly Hamming's criterion.
- Common toy-model procedure (my synthesis): (1) pick one regularity; (2) find the smallest system that reproduces it; (3) state at least two mechanisms; (4) derive a prediction under each that differs in the toy; (5) run the discriminating experiment; (6) extract a quantity (exponent, phase boundary, progress measure) and check it on the real system; (7) label the evidence strength honestly.

- No author's first-person account of how the question was chosen was found for any of these papers; the "what made it good" analysis is inferred from the papers' structure, not from the authors' stated process.
- Scaling-law theory beyond the quantization model (e.g., Bahri et al., Sharma & Kaplan, Hutter) was not researched within the call budget.
